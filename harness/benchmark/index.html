Benchmark

This is the record of every test run on the closed loop, kept so that a reader who doubts it can check. The closed loop is a set of tools that hold a coding agent’s work to written requirements: it breaks the code on purpose, in small ways, and asks whether a test notices. It claims to make an agent’s changes match what was asked. Whether that is true is what this page is for. Each entry gives the question a test asked, the bar it had to clear and whether the bar was fixed before the result, how it could have failed, the command that runs it again, the result read from the file that holds it, and what it cost.

Where it stands today

55 tests are on record. 35 passed, 12 failed, 8 are unclear and 0 are not finished. A test passing here means a stated bar was met on stated data. It does not mean the loop is proven right.

What failed

  • How full must the graph be before the network beats the plain rules?: On three of four graphs the network did not stay ahead of the best plain rule. On one graph it did, from half full.
  • Do fresh reviewers find holes the loop passes?: The first review ranked 9 holes and the second 10. The criteria were not complete, and the reviews are why they are more now.
  • Every break of the learned parts, with a verdict: Only 2 of the 6 learned parts reach 80 percent caught, and 642 breaks got past every test.
  • Do the plan's switches hold at more seeds?: Seed 0 was the best of ten on the first project, so the public 64 percent was the top of a range. The honest figure was 50 percent. On the second project, seed 0 alone put the switch off.
  • Does each learned part beat plain rules on the loop's own code?: None of the six beat a plain rule on its own code. Four lost and two were too thin to say.
  • Can a goal be turned into a vector that points at goals worth training?: A weak direction for learnable, none for useful. It failed its bars and no page was made from it.
  • A full pass on the game feature the loop was first built on: 974 of 1,104 breaks caught, 55 survived and 77 holes of the loop's own stand, so the pass exits 1.
  • A pass on the loop's own code: 989 of 1,637 breaks caught, 648 survived, 387 holes of the loop's own stand.
  • Does the loop recover a lost model better, with the ids removed?: With the ids removed both arms recovered 16 of 24. The 19 against 10 claim was withdrawn from the deck and manual.
  • Does the loop find more defects than careful reading?: No grader found a real fault in only one arm. The loop added no defect and cost more.
  • Joining two finished features, does the graph name the seams?: The graph named at best 2 of 23 real seams, and none of the 8 open or in conflict. A word search found 10, an agent reading both models 20.
  • Does the front door's queue reach more of a producer's rules than a plain list of clarifying questions?: The record works (refusals, answers, resume, no repeats) and the first five minutes take two minutes of an agent's work after a one-minute quick install. The questions do not beat asking: the first run of the tool touched none of the 12 rules, and after fixes made on seeing that, the queue still reached fewer than the plain list on both games. About half of its questions are worth asking.

What is unclear

  • Twelve agent runs, six tasks, with the loop and without it: All twelve arms passed their hidden tests, so the test did not separate the arms. The blind grading leaned to the loop, with one grader per pair, which proved too noisy.
  • Does it matter which requirements are written first?: On the one graph with many requirements, the three requirements that span the most methods gave the network what a random seed needs all 27 for. On the loop's own model no order beat the plain rule. One seed.
  • Can an agent finish a real task with the kit?: It finished and passed its hidden test, but the paired run without the loop also passed, at less cost, and the blind grader preferred the arm without it. One pair.
  • Are the documents tool's own checks held by breaks?: The first pass let 46 of 281 breaks through; after new tests the second pass caught all but one, and one more removal has not been through a further pass.
  • Three blind graders per pair instead of one: Seven pairs favoured the loop, three the other arm and one split. Three graders agreed on only 7 of 11 pairs, and the tasks are small.
  • Does every tool have a selftest, and does it pass?: Every selftest that ran passed in the last measurement, 60 of 60, taken by benchmark.py --survey-selftests on 2026-10-31 on a copy of the tools (72 tools in all); the measurement before it was 60 of 60, and the one before that 57 of 57, taken by prove_all.py with the selftests running several at once. In the measurement before, autobot/dictation_proof.py had exited 1 while X5 was still being built. A passing selftest says little about how much it checks, and 12 tools have none.
  • Did each standing tick find something worth its minutes?: All five ticks said yes about themselves. That is five opinions of the party that did the work.
  • A real argument chain on the kit's example: One step, one run, and no bar fixed first. It shows the checks run end to end on a real step and that the loop still holds; it does not show they catch a wrong one.

What is not known

  • Whether a person outside the project can install and run it from nothing, and how long that takes.
  • How many documents describe a requirement in words the rules cannot see.
  • Whether the public numbers stay true when a record changes.
  • Which parts of the loop earn their keep.
  • How much better the loop is than not using it, for each stat.

Words used on this page
break
A small deliberate change to the code, such as flipping a comparison, made to see whether any test fails.
caught, survived
A break is caught when a test fails because of it, and survives when every test still passes.
kill rate
The share of the breaks that were caught.
hole of ours
A place where the loop's own checking lets a mistake through, and the fix is the loop's to make.
bar
The result a test must reach to count as passed, written down before the result is known. Whether it was is shown on every entry.
plant
A failure put in on purpose to show that a check can notice it.
plain rule
A simple rule that does not learn, such as counting past catches. A learned part has to beat the best one to earn a place.
network
The small learned model that guesses which requirement owns a piece of code.
seed
One random start of a run. Results are given over several seeds, because one seed can flatter.

  • Whether the loop helps an agent that writes code, as opposed to one that audits. Twelve small pairs and one repeat. Larger pairs and agents writing with the hint have not been run.
  • The manual's two stated targets: 95 percent accuracy or better, and up to 90 percent fewer tokens. Both are marked not measured in the manual. No run has produced either number.
  • An outside judgement of whether the standing ticks were worth their minutes. Each tick answered yes about itself. No one else has judged.
  • Whether any stat other than the owner network's score is followed through every link of the loop. One worsening of the owner score was planted on a copy and 9 of 10 links held (E1-chain). kill_rate has a trigger and no goal reads it, and 45 stats of the pass log have no goal. Link 5, the work a pick names being launched and its end logged, is not wired for any stat.
  • Tests run before 26 October on the earlier tools of the project. The earlier use-case and coverage tools were tested in other ways. They are not backfilled here.
